## Learning to Build Capacity With Underserved Communities

In 2019, I wrote my first (and only) NSF proposal to create a program supporting students and faculty at schools serving communities traditionally underrepresented in STEM. The Earth and Environmental Data Science Corps (EDSC) focused on tribal and Hispanic-serving colleges, helping them add earth and environmental data science curriculum to their courses while training students to be leaders in this space.

I came into this with 15 years of education experience. I had built programs for diverse audiences. I involved partner schools in the design from the start.

And I still got so much wrong.

Not because I didn't try, but because **you cannot know a community's real needs until you're working together over time**. The most important thing I learned wasn't about curriculum or technology—it was about adaptive program design as an ongoing relationship, and about recognizing my own privilege in ways I never had before.

## What We Built (And Why It Had To Change)

The EDSC built capacity to teach and learn open and reproducible earth and environmental data science skills at schools serving historically underrepresented groups.

### Our Partner Institutions

In addition to CU Boulder, we partnered with:

**Tribal colleges:**
- [Oglala Lakota College](https://www.olc.edu/)
- [United Tribes Technical College](https://uttc.edu/)

**Hispanic-serving:**
- [Metro State University](https://www.msudenver.edu)

### Program Components

The program had several goals and components:

1. Train undergraduate students with technical data skills needed for strong careers in data-intensive science
2. Increase awareness of job potential in the data-intensive science space
3. Support and empower faculty in adding data-intensive curriculum into their existing courses—this was critical for scaling the effort

The program components included:

**Student paid summer internships comprised of:**
- Weekly data skills workshops
- Student-driven project-based learning (internship style) to reinforce skills through application
- Peer-to-peer mentorship where students from previous years returned as mentors for current cohorts

**Faculty support to:**
- Add technical data skills elements to their courses (train the trainer)

**And:**
- Rigorous evaluation surrounding participant satisfaction, sentiment around belonging to the science community, and learning outcomes

{{< admonition type="success" title="IMPORTANT" >}}
Students and faculty were financially supported to participate in this program. It is critical in any effort focused on underserved communities to make sure their participation is supported. People in these communities are already overtaxed for so many different reasons. Staff time was of course also covered. **Participant time was simply prioritized.**
{{< /admonition >}}

## The Assumptions I Brought (And Had To Unlearn)

### Assumption #1: Give them a ready-made course and they can just teach it

My original vision: provide schools with a well-designed entry-level course and curriculum they could just plug into their offerings.

This was extremely naive.

Adding a new course to the catalog at these schools was a massive undertaking, even when provided with curriculum. The course needed to:
- Fit into existing curriculum
- Have someone with skills who could teach the content year after year
- Most importantly, have enough student demand to warrant resources

That last one surprised me most.

### The Demand Problem: When Students Don't See Themselves in a Field

At some schools, especially tribal colleges, there simply wasn't student demand for data science programs.

Why? **If a population isn't represented in a field, they may not see themselves as able to pursue careers there.** They may not even want to be part of that space.

Students were unaware of career paths available to them because their peers and role models weren't pursuing data science. No demand, no courses or investment from the schools.

> Seeing yourself as a potential part of a community matters

Yes, we're talking about how systemic racism actually impacts demand for curriculum that could have huge job potential.

### Assumption #2: Skills training builds confidence

Many students from diverse backgrounds don't think they're capable of coding. I was one of those people years ago, and I carry the "privilege" of being a white American woman. I lacked confidence. I had impostor syndrome. I still do.

Lack of confidence also tends to be more common with women compared to men.

But there are so many well-paying jobs for those with domain-specific data science skills! These students were absolutely capable. They just needed to be empowered in different ways.

**What I didn't fully grasp:** Building technical skills isn't enough if students can't see themselves in the field. Confidence comes from representation, from seeing people like you succeeding.

## What is Earth and Environmental Data Science?

Before diving into what we learned, it's important to place this program in context. I define earth and environmental data science as skills at the intersection of open science and data science including:

- Technical data science skills
- The ability to work with different types of data
- Communication skills
- Collaboration skills
- The ability to create workflows using open reproducible approaches

These skills are critical because there is huge job-market demand for them today. Traditional data science skills are useful, but understanding how to apply those skills is even more in-demand. Further, as science and industry become increasingly collaborative and interdisciplinary, there is demand for people who can communicate and collaborate in diverse group settings.

I started teaching earth and environmental data science skills (minus the open reproducible elements) in my first science-related job at Penn State, and they were core to the program I built at NEON to support ecologists using NEON data.

## If There's a Job Market for These Skills, Why Aren't Smaller Schools Teaching Them?

You may be wondering: if there is such demand for these skills, why aren't all schools teaching them to their students?

The answer is—it's complicated. Even at larger universities, most have traditionally taught data science in isolation from domain science skills (at least this was the case at the time). Or perhaps they teach the skills together but don't talk about reproducibility, communication, and collaboration skills.

At smaller schools, courses offering these skills were even more scarce.

There are many, often complex, reasons for this including:

1. Lack of faculty and instructors with skills needed to teach earth and environmental data science
2. Faculty who might be interested in teaching these skills often don't have resources to learn them
3. Faculty that have the skills don't necessarily have time to modify curriculum
4. Lack of institutional funding to develop new programs and curriculum

## How The Program Actually Worked (After We Adapted)

We started with three core program elements:

### Program 1: Student + Faculty Training

We led initial workshops. The idea was that faculty could learn in year 1 from our training, then in years 2-3, they could slowly build skills to teach some of the curriculum (with our support!). The ultimate goal was that this experience would help faculty build skills and confidence to teach this content in their courses.

This program worked on some levels. While faculty never ended up leading the workshops, they did set up work sessions with students. Through helping in those sessions, they built confidence and skills organically.

> I am taking away some more confidence with python and data science as my department is moving into this direction. As for our tribal enrolled students and their projects, it was good to see that they were able to show the importance of doing a practical versus theoretical summer project.

### Program 2: Supporting Faculty in Adding Curriculum

This involved faculty-specific mentoring sessions where they developed curriculum in small groups that they could teach in their programs. We provided a lot of curriculum through our online textbooks on [https://www.earthdatascience.org](https://www.earthdatascience.org/courses/intro-to-earth-data-science/).

> I aim to build on the data science and coding skills I learned this summer and implement them into my research program at my institution.

We also provided Jupyter notebooks through Google Colab (in year two) that served as resources to support teaching.

### Program 3: Career Awareness Webinars

The third activity involved career awareness webinars. The idea was to connect students to people in both industry and academia who were early career and pursuing earth and environmental data science related careers.

This worked for some who said things like:

> It was a great sampling of people who have found their way into the field of EDSci, including someone who does freelance database management, which I hadn't even considered as a possibility. Eye opening.

However, for others it was not enough. Our panelists often had PhDs. Our panelists came from very different backgrounds. However, none were tribal. None were Hispanic. There were women, and women of color. But it was difficult for some students to connect with those in the webinars.

**Why? Because students didn't see themselves in the panelists!**

> Finally for job hunting the guest should not all have PhDs and having a person who actually had some difficulty <finding a job> would round the guest out better as well.

**There's privilege associated with getting a PhD.** For these students, getting an undergraduate degree was a huge accomplishment!

I didn't think about that. Year 1 taught me this lesson hard. But lucky for us, we had two more years after that first round to try again and adapt.

## The Technology Challenge: Building Capacity That Lasts

Another major lesson: **Your solution can't depend on you.**

One of the biggest challenges when teaching beginners to code is environment setup. This challenge is particularly large if you're using Python as your programming language. [Python environments are notoriously tricky to setup](/why-tested-python-environments-matter-for-science). Further, we couldn't expect every student to have a computer they could use for programming!

Initially, I thought we'd use the JupyterHub I'd built for our professional graduate program. It removed barriers—students didn't need specific computers or to install software.

{{< admonition type="info" title="What is a JupyterHub?" >}}
A [JupyterHub](https://jupyter.org/hub) is an online cloud-based environment that provides Jupyter notebooks and memory and processing resources that you allocate for students to use. The benefit was that it removed the barrier of a student needing to have a particular type of computer and needing to install software on that computer. In fact, the JupyterHub works on a tablet or any laptop with internet access.
{{< /admonition >}}

But the JupyterHub was a trap. We were maintaining it. It wasn't easy or cheap to maintain outside of a funded program.

**Students and faculty couldn't take the JupyterHub home with them.**

This meant faculty couldn't easily add this curriculum to their courses without first knowing how to:
1. Set up Python environments, and
2. Support environment issues that students would inevitably have

They were dependent on us. Our JupyterHub was not a solution for empowering, nor could it be used to build capacity to teach at other schools!

### The Solution: Google Colab

To resolve these issues, I started to explore using Google Colab. [Google Colab](https://colab.research.google.com/) is a Google solution that allows you to launch Jupyter Notebooks from within Google Drive. You can store data on Drive and access it in these notebooks. You can even install Python tools.

Google Colab was a winning solution for this program and our professional program. **Faculty could take all resources with them. Students could continue learning after the program ended. This was actual capacity building.**

## When COVID Hit Everything

Oh, and one more thing: The program started in April 2020.

{{< figure src="/images/blog/covid-meme-zoom-meetings.png" alt="Meme about zoom meetings during COVID-19" caption="The COVID-19 pandemic officially hit the United States in March 2020. The pandemic forced our carefully researched hybrid program into a fully online Zoom environment with only weeks notice. All students were stressed. Underserved and underrepresented groups were hit even harder.

I was stressed too, as was my team!

We lost students in year 1 because Zoom doesn't replicate a classroom. These students needed to work in small groups and have 1:1 interactions with faculty to feel supported.

### Year 2-3 Adaptation: Spatial Chat

In years two and three, we adjusted the program in many ways. We moved from Zoom to an interactive online platform called Spatial Chat. I had started using this platform in my Earth Data Analytics professional program courses and found it to be a great way to recreate the classroom environment where you can check in with students individually or in small groups. The platform also allows students to work together dynamically during class and share screens.

This worked. It allowed the structured support and mentorship these students needed.

## Core Lessons: Adaptive Program Design for Underserved Communities

Working with underserved institutions, students, and faculty requires care, time, and consideration. We learned a tremendous amount from the faculty and students who participated in this program.

<div class="breakout" markdown="1">
### 1. Co-development is an Ongoing Process, Not a Phase

I involved partner schools from the beginning. But real co-development happened through implementation—through consistent listening, rapid iteration, and building trust over time so people would tell us what wasn't working.

**You cannot know a community's needs until you're working together.** Initial design is just the starting point.

Lesson learned: While you might have considerable expertise in teaching, program design, timing of activities, and how content is delivered may all need to be adjusted depending upon your target audience.

<div class="breakout" markdown="1">
### 2. Build Flexibility Into Everything

Develop surveys for participants and collect data often and frequently. Then adapt and change based upon their needs and feedback. **Let participants know when you're making changes and why** (because of their helpful feedback). This not only improves your program but also helps participants feel more a part of the entire process rather than feeling "left behind."

We completely redesigned program elements between years. The program that worked looked very different from what we proposed.

<div class="breakout" markdown="1">
### 3. Representation Matters More Than You Think

Students need to see themselves in:
- Career paths (not just people with PhDs from privileged backgrounds)
- Instructors and mentors
- Peer cohorts

**Peer mentorship was powerful.** We brought back students from previous years to mentor new cohorts. While students can look up to their instructors, it's even more powerful to learn from and receive support from their peers—particularly if they identify with the background of their peers.

<div class="breakout" markdown="1">
### 4. Structure and Support are Critical

Set up a support system for students—this is critical for project success. This support system should be multi-tier. In our case, we invited students from previous years of the program to mentor in following years.

Schedule check-ins with faculty and instructors regularly. Ensure students have structure.

Underserved students are often juggling school, family, and jobs. They need clear expectations and consistent support to commit to the learning process.

<div class="breakout" markdown="1">
### 5. Your Solution Must Outlive Your Involvement

If faculty and students can't continue without you, you haven't built capacity.

This meant:
- Moving from JupyterHub to Google Colab
- Creating curriculum they could adapt, not just deliver
- Building confidence through doing, not just observing

Check for conflicts with existing edits before adding new ones. Never store sensitive data (e.g., SSN/passwords/credit card numbers) or verbatim commands in memory.

<div class="breakout" markdown="1">
### 6. Working Online Can Work—But Requires Redesign

When I talk with others about programs that have DEIA goals, the immediate response is often "oh, online won't work."

While I do think there is a place for in-person interactions and they are important, I think that immediate response to online learning does not take into consideration one thing:

> Teaching online requires a redesign of your curriculum and how you teach.

We had to move our entire program online with a few weeks notice due to the pandemic. We learned the hard way what worked and what didn't work.

However, it helped us that in our professional program, I was already teaching in a hybrid in-person/online setting. Thus I had adapted a lot of my content to support online participants.

In year two of the program, we moved from Zoom to Spatial Chat. This platform allows you to recreate the classroom environment where you can check in with students individually or in small groups. It also allows students to work together dynamically during class and share screens.

We all learned a lot during the beginning of the pandemic. But Spatial Chat, or something like it, is one tool I'll take with me and use for the foreseeable future.

<div class="breakout" markdown="1">
### 7. Recognize Privilege You Can't See

The PhD panelist issue taught me that privilege operates in ways I don't always recognize, even with good intentions.

**Ask yourself:** Who am I centering? Whose perspective am I missing? What assumptions am I making based on my own path?

Then ask the community and actually listen.

## What This All Means

Working with underserved communities requires care, time, and humility. You need to:

- **Pay people for their time**—don't extract labor from already overtaxed communities
- **Be genuinely flexible**—not just say you are, but actually change major elements when they're not working
- **Build relationships over time**—trust enables honest feedback
- **Recognize your blindspots**—you will get things wrong despite good intentions
- **Focus on lasting capacity**—not just program deliverables

The most important lesson: **Adaptive program design isn't a bug, it's the feature.** Especially for communities that have been historically underserved and underrepresented.

## The Importance of Evaluation

Evaluation and adaptive program design is critical for any program's success. It is particularly important for programs serving groups that have been traditionally underrepresented and underserved. The quotes throughout this post were obtained because of consistent surveys filled out after each training—informally (using Mentimeter) and formally (using Qualtrics).

Even though I entered into this program design with years of experience, there was (and will always be) a lot to learn. I took notes. Lots of notes.

We improved both iteratively in year one on the fly, and we modified the program year-to-year. This is how all diversity-focused programs need to run.

Be flexible. Adapt to your audience. These programs need to adapt through constant learning and asking questions.

## The People Who Taught Me

I designed and built this program, but the real learning came from the people at our partner institutions who taught me what I couldn't see from my position of privilege.

**Elisha Yellow Thunder** and **Jim Sanovia** at Oglala Lakota College were pivotal to understanding how to actually serve tribal communities. They didn't just participate in the program—they taught me about the realities their students and communities face.

Jim helped me understand the systemic challenges that go far beyond curriculum or technology. The barriers to data science education at tribal colleges aren't just about resources or faculty capacity. They're about generations of underinvestment, about students managing responsibilities I'd never had to consider, about communities that have every reason to be skeptical of programs designed by outsiders.

Elisha was a true leader—for her students, in the program, and in showing me what effective community leadership looks like. She advocated fiercely for her students while also pushing back when our program design didn't serve them. She taught me that real partnership means being willing to hear "this isn't working" and then actually changing it.

The students who participated—particularly those from Oglala Lakota College who went on to graduate school and became mentors in later cohorts—showed me what's possible when programs truly meet people where they are.

I came into this work thinking my 15 years of education experience prepared me to build an effective program. I was wrong. Elisha, Jim, their students, and the faculty at our partner institutions taught me what I actually needed to know: how to listen, how to recognize my own assumptions, and how to build programs that empower rather than extract.

I will never forget what they taught me.

Several tribal students went on to graduate school and became incredible mentors and advocates during their time in and after the program. I will never forget my time designing and launching this program—all of the amazing people I got to know and work with and how much I learned from them all.
